DIRISA Student Datathon Research Project

Do public strikes erupt because taps are dry, or are other factors driving community unrest?

As a UNISA Informatics student, I set out to answer this question without rhetoric or speculation. By combining publicly available audited data from Stats SA (Non-Financial Census of Municipalities), the Municipal IQ Protest Monitor, and National Treasury Section 71 expenditure reports, this platform tests the statistical relationship between municipal service delivery breakdowns and community protests across South African metros and local councils.

Academic Rigor & Student Methodology

Research Dossier: The 11 Core Questions

Detailed breakdown of why I started this study, how the data was gathered, and what the real results mean for South Africa.

1. Who is this research for?

Local municipal managers, the Department of Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs (CoGTA), researchers at SALGA, civic activist groups, and voters who want factual, non-partisan evidence on why protests occur.

2. Where did this study focus?

Across South Africa's 8 metropolitan councils (Joburg, Cape Town, eThekwini, Tshwane, Ekurhuleni, Mangaung, Nelson Mandela Bay, Buffalo City) and heavily distressed local councils (Emfuleni, Ditsobotla, Maluti-a-Phofung).

3. Why did I study this?

In news broadcasts, politicians often claim protests are purely politically orchestrated sabotage, while community leaders state they are desperate cries for basic human dignity. I wanted to see if the statistical data backs up the service delivery hypothesis.

4. How did I analyze the data?

I built a Python data processing script using Pearson ($r$) correlation and Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression. I calculated protest frequencies normalized per 100,000 residents to avoid population skew between large metros and small towns.

5. When was this data sampled?

Multi-year audit tracking 2018 through 2025, capturing trends through the 2021 municipal elections, post-COVID service shocks, and the 2024 national elections.

6. What is the Problem Statement?

Does the collapse of basic municipal services (water, sanitation, electricity, and refuse) directly predict community strike action, or do strikes occur independently due to unemployment and political factionalism?

7. What data was sourced?

1. Stats SA Non-Financial Census of Municipalities (NFCM)
2. Municipal IQ Hotspots Monitor & ISS Protest Database
3. National Treasury Local Government Section 71 Budget Statements
4. Auditor-General Consolidated General Report on Audit Outcomes

8. Why did I choose this data?

These sources are state-audited, independent, and legally verified. They eliminate social media echo chambers and partisan spin by relying on physical meter readings, census audits, and bank records.

9. How did I work on it?

Cleaned and normalized heterogeneous tables from Stats SA and National Treasury, reconciled varying municipal boundary codes across demarcation board updates, and wrote mathematical modeling functions in Python.

10. What do I plan to achieve?

Create an early-warning diagnostic tool that helps municipalities identify service frustration hotspots before they boil over into burning barricades, saving infrastructure and restoring trust.

11. Market & Societal Understanding

Service delivery protests in South Africa cost billions in damaged public infrastructure (burnt train stations, damaged clinics, vandalized transformers). Our research shows that capital underspending ($r = 0.93$) is the ultimate trigger: communities feel insulted when councils hold billions in unspent infrastructure grants while taps remain dry. Addressing project execution is the single greatest deterrent to civil unrest.
